# THE ASSESSMENT JARGON BUSTER
### Plain-English definitions for the Effective Assessment Practices Workshop (KATON 2026)

**How to use this booklet:** Every technical term in the *Teacher Assessment Manual and Toolkit* (NaCCA / Ministry of Education, Ghana) is explained here in two ways — a **plain-English meaning** and a **classroom picture** you can actually see in a Ghanaian SHS. Use it as a handout, or read the "Classroom picture" column aloud during your presentation.

---

## PART 1 — THE BIG IDEA

### Assessment
**Plain English:** Finding out what a learner knows and can do — and how far they have come towards the goal you set.
**Classroom picture:** It is not only the end-of-term paper. It is every question you ask, every exercise book you mark, every time you watch a group work and think, "Ah, they have not understood this."
**Not to be confused with:** *Evaluation* (judging the overall worth of a programme) or *testing* (only one tool inside assessment).
**The official words:** "The process of collecting information on the learner to help in deciding the degree to which the learner has achieved the expected learning outcomes/standards."

### Learning Outcome
**Plain English:** A clear sentence saying what a learner should be able to *do* by the end of a lesson or unit.
**Classroom picture:** "By the end of this lesson, the learner should be able to **balance a simple chemical equation**." Not "teach chapter 4."
**Why it matters:** Assessment can only be fair if everyone — you and the learner — knows what the target is.

### Curriculum
**Plain English:** The official plan of what should be taught and learned at each level.
**Classroom picture:** The NaCCA SHS/SHTS/STEM curriculum document on your table.

### Pedagogy
**Plain English:** The method and art of teaching.
**Classroom picture:** Whether you lecture, or group them, or let them discover — that choice is your pedagogy.

## PART 2 — THE THREE SISTERS: AfL, AaL AND AoL

These three acronyms are the most confusing in the whole manual. Here is the simplest way to hold them.

| Acronym | Full name | Who is doing the work? | Plain English |
|---|---|---|---|
| **AfL** | Assessment **for** Learning | **The teacher** | I check as we go, so I can teach better. |
| **AaL** | Assessment **as** Learning | **The learner** | The student checks themselves, so they can learn better. |
| **AoL** | Assessment **of** Learning | **The system / teacher** | A final summary of what has been achieved. |

### Formative Assessment (AfL + AaL)
**Plain English:** Checking learning **while** teaching is still going on, so you can fix things before it is too late.
**Classroom picture:** **Tasting the soup while it is still on the fire.** You do not wait until the food is served before you know there is no salt in it. You taste, you adjust, you taste again.
**The official words:** "Used formally and informally to gather information about learners and their learning needs to enable teachers to modify or improve teaching and learning."
**Key word to remember:** *Improve* — not *prove*.

### Assessment for Learning (AfL)
**Plain English:** The teacher gathers evidence during learning and changes what they do next.
**Classroom picture:** Halfway through a lesson on the mole concept, you ask for a show of hands and see blank faces. You stop and re-teach with a different example.

### Assessment as Learning (AaL)
**Plain English:** The learner takes charge of checking their own understanding.
**Classroom picture:** A student keeps a small notebook where, after each lesson, they write one thing they understood and one thing that is still confusing them.

### Summative Assessment / Assessment of Learning (AoL)
**Plain English:** A formal, planned check **at the end** to sum up overall achievement — for promotion, certification or placement.
**Classroom picture:** **Serving the food at the table.** The cooking is finished. This is the meal as it is. You cannot add salt now.
**The official words:** "A formal and planned assessment to gather information on learners' overall learning achievements at the end of teaching and learning or term or year."
**Examples:** End-of-term exams, WASSCE, end-of-programme exams.

### The golden rule (and the one teachers miss)
> **Summative results can be used formatively, and formative work can count summatively.**

- **Using summative for formative:** You mark the end-of-term paper but you *don't* just score it. You give it back, learners discuss in groups the questions they got wrong, and you re-teach the weak topics next term. The exam just became a teaching tool.
- **Using formative for summative:** Class exercises and project work graded against a clear rubric become part of the 30% internal assessment. The everyday work just became part of the grade.

## PART 3 — THE OTHER FORMS OF ASSESSMENT

### Diagnostic Assessment
**Plain English:** Checking what learners **already know and can do** *before* you start teaching, so you know where to begin.
**Classroom picture:** **Checking your pantry before you start cooking.** Have you got the rice? The oil? Is the yam already boiled? Only then do you decide what to cook.
**Key word:** *Prior knowledge.*
**Timing:** Before the topic, or at the very start.
**Tools:** Pre-tests, entry tickets, K-W-L charts, concept maps, short interviews.

### Differentiated Assessment
**Plain English:** Using different ways of assessing so that every learner, whatever their level or need, has a fair chance to show what they can do.
**Classroom picture:** Your WASSCE-repeater and your top-rated student both need to show they understand photosynthesis. One writes the essay, one draws and labels the diagram and explains it orally. Both prove the same learning outcome.
**The manual's six levers:** varied formats, flexible deadlines, varying task difficulty, accommodations, individualised feedback, learner involvement.
**Do not confuse with:** *Lowering the standard.* You change the **route**, never the **destination**.

### Performance-Based / Practical / Authentic Assessment
**Plain English:** Learners **actually do** something real — not just write about it.
**Classroom picture:** You do not ask them to describe a titration on paper. They stand at the bench and titrate. You do not ask them to list the parts of a machine. They dismantle and reassemble it.
**The word "authentic"** just means: **it looks like real life.**
**Examples:** Experiments, exhibitions/STEM fairs, fieldwork reports, projects, drama performances, debate, simulation, oral/aural assessment.

### e-Assessment
**Plain English:** Using technology and digital tools to assess learning.
**Classroom picture:** It does **not** need a computer lab. A class WhatsApp group where learners submit a voice-note explaining a concept; a Google Form quiz marked automatically; a Kahoot round at the end of a lesson.
**The manual's examples:** Online quizzes, digital portfolios, virtual labs, discussion boards, e-open-book exams, multimedia projects, learning analytics, digital rubrics.
**Low-tech reality check:** Where internet is a challenge, WhatsApp, SMS and offline Google Forms still count as e-assessment.

## PART 4 — THE SEVEN PRINCIPLES (SECTION 2 OF THE MANUAL)

Think of these as **seven pillars holding up the roof of your assessment practice.** If one is missing, the building leans.

### 1. Validity
**Plain English:** Are you measuring **the right thing**?
**Classroom picture:** **Using a scale to measure weight, not height.** If your learning outcome is "the learner should be able to *analyse* data," but your test only asks them to *recall* the definition, the test is **not valid** — no matter how neatly it is marked.
**Quick validity check:** "Does this question actually test the skill I said I was teaching?"

### 2. Reliability
**Plain English:** Do you get the **same result every time**, whoever is marking?
**Classroom picture:** **A market scale that gives the same weight for the same pile of tomatoes, whether it is Monday or Saturday, and whoever is standing at the stall.** If this term's 30 marks in your subject are generous and next term's are harsh, the scores are not reliable.
**The manual's warning:** The *"I know it when I see it"* approach is **not acceptable.** If you cannot put your criteria into words, you cannot be reliable.
**How to build it:** Use a proper marking scheme, use rubrics with weightings, use more than one assessment method for the same outcome, and let a colleague review your marking.

### 3. Fairness and Ethics
**Plain English:** Giving every learner an **equitable chance** to show what they can do — and never using a task that unfairly favours or disadvantages someone.
**Classroom picture:** An assessment question about the rules of cricket will unfairly fail a learner from a village that has never played cricket. A question about *a familiar Ghanaian market scene* will not. Avoid culturally biased content, unfamiliar words and discriminatory examples.
**Also covers:** Telling learners the format in advance, giving extra time and alternative formats to learners with Special Educational Needs (SEN), and not assessing what you have not taught.

### 4. Transparency
**Plain English:** Learners must know **what they will be judged on and how.**
**Classroom picture:** **Showing the rubric before the project starts**, not after. Never make assessment a secret exam where learners are guessing what the teacher wants.
**Also means:** Telling learners the pass mark, sharing results with learners and parents/guardians openly, and giving learners a route to seek review and redress if they disagree with a score.

### 5. Inclusivity
**Plain English:** Assessment that is fair and accessible to **ALL** learners.
**Classroom picture:** Your learner with low vision gets a large-print paper. Your learner with dyslexia gets extra time. Your learner who is hard of hearing has the instructions written, not just spoken.
**The manual's acronyms here:**
- **GESI** = *Gender Equality and Social Inclusion* — no learner is disadvantaged by gender, disability, poverty or background.
- **SEL** = *Socio-Emotional Learning* — attending to the learner's feelings, confidence and relationships, not only their marks.
- **SEN** = *Special Educational Needs* — learners requiring specific support.
**Practical tools:** Braille, oral translation, text-to-speech, sign language interpretation, assistive technology.
**A caution:** Make sure rubrics don't punish what isn't being assessed. If you are marking *science understanding*, don't dock marks for handwriting.

### 6. Practicability
**Plain English:** Can you **actually do it**, with the time, materials and class size you really have?
**Classroom picture:** A beautifully designed one-hour one-on-one oral assessment for a class of 60 pupils is **not practicable.** It will collapse by Thursday.
**The three questions:** Do I have the materials? Does the format suit this class size and level? Do I have the **time** to set it, mark it, and give feedback?

### 7. Assessment Utility
**Plain English:** Is this assessment actually **useful**? Does it give you and the learner something worth having?
**Classroom picture:** You set the test, mark it, record the scores and file the papers away. Did **anybody learn anything** from it? If not, the utility was almost zero. Utility means the information leads to **action** — a re-teach, a targeted intervention, a change in seating, a conversation with a parent.
**The manual's checklist:** State the intended use of the result, give constructive feedback, and provide credible information to learners and parents.

## PART 5 — TOOLS AND TECHNIQUES, IN PLAIN WORDS

### Rubric
**Plain English:** A table showing exactly what counts and how many marks each part is worth, at each level.
**Classroom picture:** **A recipe card with measurements.** Not "add enough salt" but "add one teaspoon." It removes the guesswork for the learner and the bias from the marker.
**Tip:** Give the rubric **before** the task, not after.

### Marking Scheme
**Plain English:** The model answer sheet showing where each mark falls.
**Classroom picture:** Your colleague should be able to mark your paper and produce almost the same scores as you did.

### Table of Specification ("test blueprint")
**Plain English:** A planning grid that makes sure your test covers the content and the thinking levels it is supposed to.
**Classroom picture:** Instead of 20 questions that are all recall because recall is easy to set, the grid forces you to allocate marks across topics and across Depth of Knowledge levels. **This is the single best defence of validity.**

### Depth of Knowledge (DoK)
**Plain English:** How **deep** the thinking is — not how hard the question *looks*. There are four levels.
**Classroom picture:** Four steps up a staircase.

| Level | Name | Plain English | SHS Example (Social Studies) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | Recall / Reproduction | Give back a fact. | "State three causes of climate change." |
| **2** | Skills and Concepts | Use knowledge in a familiar routine. | "Explain how the greenhouse effect works, using a diagram." |
| **3** | Strategic Thinking | Reason, justify, plan — there is more than one route. | "A cocoa farmer's yield is falling. Using the data given, diagnose the likely cause and justify your answer." |
| **4** | Extended Thinking | Investigate over time, synthesise, produce something original. | "Conduct a week-long survey in your community on waste disposal and present recommendations to the assembly." |

**Key point for teachers:** A question is *not* harder just because the words are bigger. **DoK is about the thinking, not the vocabulary.**

### Portfolio
**Plain English:** A carefully chosen collection of a learner's work across time, showing progress.
**Classroom picture:** Not a folder of everything. A **curated selection** — with a reflection where the learner explains why each piece was chosen and what they improved.

### Project-Based Assessment
**Plain English:** Learners work on a meaningful, extended task and produce something real.
**Classroom picture:** Not a weekend homework. A term-long task — research, plan, produce, present, and be judged on all of it.

### Case Study
**Plain English:** Giving learners a real or realistic scenario and asking them to analyse it and decide.
**Classroom picture:** "Here are the accounts of a small market business that is failing. As a consultant, what would you advise, and why?"

### Test of Practical Knowledge (TPK)
**Plain English:** A written paper that tests your knowledge of **how to do** a practical — the theory behind the practical.
**Classroom picture:** In science, a paper asking why you add acid to the burette the way you do; what would happen if you didn't; how you would correct a reading.

### Questioning
**Plain English:** Using questions deliberately to find out what learners understand and to push their thinking deeper.
**Classroom picture:** Not only "Who can tell me...?" to the eager boy in the front row. Deliberate **wait time**, questions directed by name, and follow-ups like "Why do you say so?" and "How did you get that?"

### Think-Pair-Share
**Plain English:** Think alone → discuss with a partner → share with the whole class.
**Classroom picture:** Three minutes of genuine thinking time, so the quiet learners in the back have something to say by the time you ask.

### Think-Aloud
**Plain English:** Learners say out loud what is going on in their head as they solve a problem.
**Classroom picture:** "I am reading the question... first I will find the mass... now I'm not sure which formula to use." It exposes the *reasoning*, which a written answer conceals.

### Exit Card (or "Exit Ticket")
**Plain English:** A short question at the very end of the lesson learners answer on a slip before they leave.
**Classroom picture:** "Write one thing you learned today and one thing you are still confused about." Colleagues: this is the cheapest, fastest formative tool available and it takes three minutes.

### Entry Ticket
**Plain English:** A short question at the start of a lesson that shows what learners already bring.
**Classroom picture:** Free diagnostic assessment, every single day, at no cost.

### K-W-L Chart
**Plain English:** Three columns — **K**now, **W**ant to know, **L**earned.
**Classroom picture:** Before the topic, they fill K and W. After the topic, they fill L. It makes learning visible and it makes the learner reflective.

### Concept Map
**Plain English:** A diagram showing how ideas are linked together.
**Classroom picture:** Not a mind-map of neat bubbles only — the **arrows must be labelled** ("causes," "is part of," "depends on"). The labels are where the understanding is.

### Graphic Organiser
**Plain English:** Any visual frame that helps learners arrange their thoughts — Venn diagram, flowchart, table.
**Classroom picture:** Comparing two economic systems in a Venn diagram, where the overlap is the interesting part.

### Reflection Journal
**Plain English:** A learner's own written record of their thinking and growth, reviewed over time.
**Classroom picture:** Five minutes at the end of a double period: "What did I find hard today? What will I do differently next time?"

### Peer Assessment
**Plain English:** Learners assess each other's work against given criteria.
**Classroom picture:** Only works if they have the **rubric in hand** and have been trained how to be kind and specific. Otherwise it becomes a popularity contest.

### Self-Assessment
**Plain English:** Learners judge their own work against the criteria.
**Classroom picture:** Learner marks their own draft against the rubric, then you mark it. Where the two differ is a goldmine of teaching information.

### Metacognition
**Plain English:** Thinking about your own thinking. Knowing *how* you learn and *where* you are stuck.
**Classroom picture:** The student who can tell you, "I understand the theory but I keep making arithmetic slips," has metacognition. The student who says "I just don't get maths" does not — yet.
**Why it matters:** It is the core of **Assessment as Learning**.

### Feedback
**Plain English:** Information given to a learner that helps them close the gap between where they are and where they need to be.
**Classroom picture:** **The mechanic.** A useless mechanic says "the car is bad." A good mechanic opens the bonnet, points at one exact part and says: "This hose is cracked. Replace it and the overheating stops." **Specific, targeted, actionable.**
**The difference that matters:** Feedback is not a score, and it is not praise. "Good work, 7/10" is not feedback.
**The manual's terms:**
- **Constructive feedback** — points to the next step, not just the fault.
- **Formative use of feedback** — the learner must be given **time to act on it**. If you hand back corrections and immediately move on, the feedback dies.

### Gamification
**Plain English:** Using game elements — points, levels, badges, competition — to drive learning and assessment.
**Classroom picture:** House-based quiz with a points board; a "level up" chart on the wall where learners move up as they master each learning outcome.

### Simulation
**Plain English:** Creating a safe model of a real situation for learners to work through.
**Classroom picture:** A **Model United Nations** debate; a courtroom role-play in a Government class; a mock business negotiation in Business Management.

### Dramatisation / Dramatic Monologue
**Plain English:** Learning and being assessed through acting it out.
**Classroom picture:** A Literature learner performs a dramatic monologue as a character from *Things Fall Apart*; a History class dramatises a historical negotiation.

### Displays and Exhibitions
**Plain English:** Learners present their work publicly for an audience.
**Classroom picture:** The **STEM fair** the manual itself describes, where Robotics learners exhibit robots they dismantled and reassembled, and the school community views them.

### Checklist
**Plain English:** A simple yes/no list of what should be present.
**Classroom picture:** "Does the practical report have: aim, apparatus, method, results, conclusion?" Tick, tick, tick. Fast and reliable for skill sequences.

### Critiquing
**Plain English:** Learners give reasoned judgement on a piece of work, a performance or an argument — with justification.
**Classroom picture:** Critiquing a classmate's poster: "The layout works because the eye moves top-to-bottom, but the key is missing."

### Fieldwork / Field Report
**Plain English:** Learning out in the real environment, then writing up what was observed and analysed.
**Classroom picture:** A Biology class visiting a local river or a farm, recording species and observations, then writing a structured report.

### Learning Analytics
**Plain English:** Looking at patterns in assessment data to spot trends and act early.
**Classroom picture:** Your mark book shows that **18 out of 45 learners failed the same question on mole calculations** — a whole-class gap you can now re-teach, rather than 18 separate private problems.

### The 70 / 30 split — explained
**Plain English:** **Thirty per cent** of the learner's final grade comes from work done in your school, marked by teachers — portfolios, performance and project work, plus end-of-term exams. **Seventy per cent** comes from WAEC's final WASSCE.
**Why it matters for you:** Your internal assessment is **no longer soft data**. It goes onto a **school-based transcript** with far greater quality assurance. If your school's 30% is not credible, transparent and defensible, the learner is the one who suffers — and your school is the one that is exposed.

## PART 7 — PHRASES TEACHERS OFTEN GET WRONG

| People say... | But the manual means... |
|---|---|
| "Formative assessment is just class exercises." | Formative assessment is a **purpose** — it is any assessment used to *improve* learning, including a homework, a question, or a conversation. The tool matters less than the use. |
| "Continuous assessment means plenty of tests." | It means **a variety of evidence over time** — projects, portfolios, observations, performance, not just a bigger pile of papers. |
| "I differentiate, so I give the weak ones easier work." | Differentiation **changes the route, not the destination.** The learning outcome stays; the support and format change. |
| "The exam is over, so I just record the marks." | The manual explicitly says to **use summative results formatively** — hand it back, let them discuss the errors, and re-teach the gaps. |
| "I mark it and give it back." | Feedback without **time to act on it** is decoration. Leave space in the next lesson for them to use it. |
| "Feedback means saying 'good work'." | It means telling them **exactly where the gap is and what the next step is.** |
| "Setting multiple-choice questions is easy." | It is the most difficult item type to write well. Distractors must reflect **real misconceptions**, not random wrong answers. |

## THE ONE-PAGE SUMMARY

> **Assessment is not the exam at the end. It is the nervous system of your teaching.**
>
> **Assessment FOR learning** — the teacher listens and adjusts.
> **Assessment AS learning** — the learner reflects and adjusts.
> **Assessment OF learning** — the system records and certifies.
>
> **Before** you teach, you check what they bring (**diagnostic**).
> **While** you teach, you check and adjust (**formative**) — *you taste the soup*.
> **At the end**, you summarise (**summative**) — *you serve the meal*.
> **All the way through**, you make it **valid, reliable, fair, transparent, inclusive, practicable and useful.**
>
> And always: **the learner must know what "good" looks like, and must have time to get there.**
